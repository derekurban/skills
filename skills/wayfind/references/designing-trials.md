# Designing Trials

A trial is a bounded attempt to learn through doing. Use this guidance when the setup could produce a misleading conclusion or the next commitment is consequential.

## Make the experience adequate

Ask what must be real to answer the question. A sketch can help compare layouts. Judging interruptions needs actual timing and interaction. Checking reliability requires real executions. Label what is simulated and what remains untested.

Keep comparisons fair: use comparable tasks, competence, and polish. Do not make one alternative flawless and give its rival all the bugs. Label illustrative timings as estimates and expected reactions as predictions. Agreement among agents is not evidence of a user's experience.

## Make the result interpretable

For a substantial trial, briefly record:

- The decision or question it informs.
- What will happen, under which conditions, and within what effort limit.
- What can be observed and what the trial cannot establish.
- How useful, unfavorable, mixed, or inconclusive results affect the next move.

If exploring unfamiliar possibilities, replace a test prediction with the contrasts to examine and a point for summarizing what questions emerged.

## Avoid reading too much into reactions

“The voice prototype kept disconnecting, and I hated it” establishes dissatisfaction with that session. It does not separate dislike of voice from dislike of failures. A stable trial might help, if resolving that question is still worthwhile. Improvement would support an explanation without proving a single cause or a lasting preference.

“Predictable evenings matter most” does not establish that regularly scheduled evening meetings are acceptable. Ask what the user wants to protect before recommending a personal tradeoff. Do not turn a ranked priority into an automatic veto or permission to sacrifice it.

Some activities become meaningful only with practice. Offer enough participation for a fair judgment, with a review point and room to stop. Do not dismiss disappointment as a phase the user must endure, or treat other people as instruments for an experiment.

When further learning would not change the next responsible action, stop testing and make the choice or proceed with the work.
