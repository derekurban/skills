# Wayfind

## What this skill does

Help someone find their way when they do not yet know exactly what they want or how to get there.

Ordinary planning starts with a destination and works out the steps. Here, the destination may change as the person learns. Seeing examples, trying an activity, or building something small can reveal possibilities and preferences that conversation alone cannot.

Your job is to choose and take a useful next step, then use what happens to improve the direction. Repeat only while more exploration is worthwhile.

## How to use it

Follow this loop: **Understand → Try → Learn → Choose.** Start wherever the available context puts you; do not repeat work or print every step.

### 1. Understand where we are

Use existing context to identify:

- What the user hopes to accomplish, and what remains uncertain.
- What matters to them and which boundaries must be respected.
- Which decision needs to be made next.

Treat your guesses as guesses. A proposed solution is not necessarily the underlying goal, but do not replace it with a goal you invented. Ask a question when the answer changes the next step. If the user needs to see or try something before they can answer, provide that opportunity.

### 2. Try something that helps us move

Match the next step to what is missing:

| What we need | What to do |
| --- | --- |
| A fact or constraint | Inspect a source or the actual system. |
| Evidence that an approach works | Run a focused test. |
| A sense of what feels useful | Show contrasting options or arrange a realistic trial. |
| Enough understanding to judge | Explain, demonstrate, or practice with feedback. |
| Better questions or possibilities | Explore a few meaningfully different examples. |
| A choice between competing values | Clarify the tradeoff and what the user will accept. |

When the questions are still forming, exploration can produce a better question rather than test a prediction. Give it a scope and a stopping point.

Choose the least costly step that can teach us enough to affect a decision or enable later learning. Consider alternatives, including keeping things as they are. Check important assumptions before expensive commitments. Sometimes learning one thing makes the next question answerable.

Make the immediate action concrete, the next steps conditional on its result, and the distant plan provisional. For a substantial trial, state what it should teach us, its effort limit, and what it cannot establish.

Do the work you can perform within the user's authorization. When it requires their experience, prepare something usable and wait for their reaction. If access is missing, say what remains undone.

### 3. Learn from what happened

Separate **what happened**, **what it might mean**, and **what changes now**. Keep observations distinct from predictions, interpretations, and the user's own statements. Do not invent results or reactions.

Match conclusions to the evidence. A convincing demonstration does not prove reliability. A frustrating broken prototype does not settle whether the underlying idea is worthwhile. An inconclusive result is allowed.

Listen to reported enjoyment, boredom, or discomfort without diagnosing the user. These may reflect fit, unfamiliarity, or problems with the trial. Let the user interpret what matters to them; do not assume what tradeoff they will accept.

Update both the route and the destination when warranted. Experience can change what someone wants as well as help them express it.

### 4. Choose what happens next

Continue exploring, change direction, commit to action, or stop. Gather more evidence only when it is worth the cost for the next decision. Once the direction is sufficiently clear, carry out the requested work instead of reopening discovery.

Some experiences need sustained participation. Choose a fair review point rather than constantly reconsidering; respect a decision to stop. Keep remaining uncertainties visible without making them all prerequisites for action.

If work spans sessions, save a short task note: current direction, user-stated boundaries, evidence and its limits, open questions, and next action. Preserve the difference between what the user said and what you inferred.

## A simple example

“I want an AI coding partner, but I don't know how involved I want to be.”

Create two short examples of the same task: one with checkpoints and one with delegated decisions. The user reacts to where they want control or explanation. Use that reaction to select a real trial. The examples help reveal a useful distinction; they do not establish how a working system will feel or perform.

## Keep it useful

Lead with the result or next action and a brief reason. Use only as much process as the task needs. For comparisons, misleading results, or consequential trials, read [designing useful trials](references/probe-judgment.md).
